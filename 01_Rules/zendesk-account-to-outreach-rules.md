# Zendesk Account-to-Outreach Rules Sheet

## Core Rule

Always research the account before selecting prospects.

The account research determines:

- What the company is trying to accomplish
- Which operational pressures may create urgency
- Which business functions own those pressures
- Which prospect titles are worth targeting
- Which Zendesk capability may be relevant

LinkedIn research comes after account research. It personalizes the message. It does not create the reason for outreach.

## Winning Style Principles

Use the most effective traits from the Zach examples without copying Zach's voice or weakening the account-led standard.

Every message should make the recipient understand three things quickly:

- Why this account or situation is relevant
- What operational issue may be worth examining
- Why replying requires little effort

The preferred tone is:

- Conversational, calm, and curious
- Specific without pretending to know more than the evidence supports
- Low-pressure without sounding vague or apologetic
- Direct enough to invite a real answer

Use this central message pattern:

> You may already be dealing with this issue. We have relevant experience. Is it worth comparing notes?

Strong reasons to reach out include:

- A verified account initiative that may create operational pressure
- A prior conversation or stalled evaluation
- Existing Zendesk use by another division, brand, or related company
- A credible customer example matched to the prospect's problem
- A relevant professional or personal connection that supports the opening

These reasons support relevance. They do not replace an operating hypothesis.

Prefer one clear idea per email. A short message with a specific question is stronger than a longer message containing several capabilities, claims, or angles. The message should sound like a person who understands the business situation, not a sender explaining the recipient’s job.

Use permission-based language when it lowers resistance, such as “No pitch, just context-gathering.” Use it only when the message itself supports that claim. Do not use low-pressure language to disguise a product pitch.

Treat response patterns as hypotheses to test, not proof of causation. A response does not by itself prove that a subject line, tone, proof point, or timing caused the result.

Use the two-person test: if the same line could be sent unchanged to two people, it is relevance, not one-to-one personalization. Relevance can still be useful. Do not label a segmented observation as personal research.

Every personal or account detail must bridge to the operating problem. If it does not help explain why that problem may exist for this person, cut it.

Use person-specific details such as a recent role move, public post, career pattern, or documented responsibility when they sharpen the business point. Do not use a title as a substitute for understanding. Avoid role-description phrases such as “your role covers” and similar language that tells the recipient what they already know about their job.

Use first-person language when it makes the note more human or makes the sender’s point of view useful. “I noticed,” “I have seen,” and “I may be looking at the wrong problem” are appropriate when they lead to a specific account or operating observation. Do not use first person to narrate research or prospecting mechanics.

### Direct-conversation and cut-through standard

Cold email is a conversation addressed to one person, not an account brief converted into a greeting. Read every draft aloud as if it were the opening of a phone call. If the sender could not naturally say it to the recipient, revise it.

Use personal pronouns naturally:

- “I” should make the sender’s observation or uncertainty accountable.
- “You” or “your” should connect the issue to the recipient’s operating reality.
- “We” should be rare and should not imply an existing relationship.

Do not force pronouns to meet a quota. However, a draft that contains neither a natural sender perspective nor direct recipient language is a QA warning. It usually reads like research notes, a company description, or a message written about the recipient rather than to them.

To cut through high-volume inboxes, every first touch must earn attention with one distinctive, concrete observation that would be difficult to reuse unchanged for another company or person. Distinctive does not mean clever, flattering, longer, or more dramatic. It means the observation changes the business question.

The goal is not to exploit a suspected pain. The goal is to test whether a public signal creates a real operating consequence for this person. State the tension as a hypothesis, connect it to what the recipient may see or decide, and ask a question that lets them confirm, correct, redirect, or dismiss it.

Do not claim that the account is worse than competitors without a verified comparison using the same metric, period, scope, and source quality. Without that evidence, ask where inconsistency, delay, rework, or missed visibility may exist rather than manufacturing a performance gap.

## Phase 1: Account Research

Start with the company, not the people.

Research:

- Current strategic initiatives
- Growth plans
- Digital, AI, or technology programs
- Operating model changes
- Expansion, restructuring, or refranchising
- Customer or employee experience priorities
- Logistics, supply chain, or service changes
- Public risks, performance pressure, or transformation goals
- Relevant annual reports, earnings materials, job postings, press releases, and company pages

Separate every finding into:

- Verified fact
- Reasonable inference
- Unknown or unverified

Do not turn an inference into a claim.

## Phase 2: Signal Map

Reduce the account research to three to five strong signals.

For each signal, define:

- What the company is doing
- Why it may create operational pressure
- Which business function owns that pressure
- Which prospect titles likely influence it
- Which Zendesk capability may be relevant
- What evidence supports the signal

Use the strongest signals first. A strategic initiative with a clear owner is stronger than a generic company fact.

## Phase 3: Prospect Selection

Only after the signal map is complete, prospect on LinkedIn.

Look for people who:

- Own the function connected to the signal
- Have a title matching the business problem
- Recently took on responsibility related to the initiative
- Mention relevant programs, teams, systems, or operating priorities
- Have enough authority or influence to care about the issue

Capture:

- Name
- Current title
- Company
- Tenure
- Function
- Relevant profile language
- Recent activity
- Career background
- Likely business pressure
- Signal-to-person connection
- Confidence level

Do not select prospects only because they are senior. Select them because their role connects to a verified account signal.

## Phase 4: Prospect-to-Signal Matching

Give every prospect one primary angle.

Use this format:

- Account signal:
- Prospect responsibility:
- Likely pressure:
- Zendesk angle:
- Evidence:
- Confidence:
- Outreach direction:

Do not give one prospect several unrelated angles. One clear business issue produces stronger outreach.

## Phase 5: First Email

Write Email 1 as a cold, first-touch message.

Required structure:

1. Greeting
2. Specific account or role signal
3. Plausible operational pressure
4. Soft question that invites a reply

Email 1 rules:

- Default to a product-neutral, proof-free Email 1
- Treat Zendesk timing and proof timing as testable hypotheses, not universal laws
- In a controlled vendor-present test, use at most one short sentence to name Zendesk, one relevant capability, or one matched proof point
- Use the vendor-present test only when it clarifies the connection between the account signal and the operating problem
- Do not combine a capability and proof point or list features in Email 1
- Do not ask for a meeting
- Do not use a product pitch
- Do not state an unverified pain as fact
- Aim for 50 to 100 words in the body
- Do not remove decision-useful, verified context solely to meet the word range
- When additional context is necessary to make the observation credible, Email 1 may extend to 140 words; keep one clear idea and one question
- When volume supports a useful test, compare 35 to 60 words with 70 to 95 words, and when justified 100 to 140 words, inside the same persona, offer, and channel
- Use one first-touch subject for the entire email thread. Do not create a new subject for each follow-up email.
- Include the prospect's first name in the subject.
- Pair the name with one specific account signal, operating tension, or role-relevant question.
- Keep the subject between two and five words by default; allow up to seven when the added words create real recipient relevance.
- Use one clear idea and make the subject credible across the full thread.
- Do not treat the prospect's name alone as personalization. The second half must carry a concrete business reason to open.
- Make the message specific enough that removing the name would weaken it
- Use a reply-focused CTA

## Phase 6: Follow-Up Sequence

### Email 1

Signal, pressure, and curiosity.

Default to no vendor name, no proof point, and no meeting ask. A controlled vendor-present test may use one short, relevant sentence under the Email 1 rules above.

Email 1 should sound like a relevant business question, not a prospecting sequence. Lead with account context, state the likely consequence, and ask one diagnostic question.

The first email should sound personally written for the recipient. Combine one specific observation, one plausible business tension, and one useful question. Do not explain the recipient’s job back to them or ask a broad question about their function.

Apply the direct-conversation test before delivery: the message should include natural first-person or second-person language where useful, sound normal when spoken, and make the recipient feel addressed rather than analyzed. If it sounds like a research summary, profile recap, or generic account hypothesis, revise it.

### Email 2

Introduce a new operational implication or, when it genuinely helps, one proof point matched to the prospect’s pressure. Include enough account and role context for the note to make sense on its own. Proof timing is a testable hypothesis, not a universal law.

Email 2 must stand alone. Do not reference the earlier email, say “following up,” or write a fragment that assumes the recipient remembers the first touch.

Keep the CTA soft.

Use a verified relationship, prior conversation, or customer example when it materially strengthens relevance. Do not add proof merely to make the email feel more credible.

### Email 3

Name Zendesk only when the account hypothesis still holds. Reintroduce the relevant business context, connect Zendesk to the prospect’s business issue, and describe the practical support or fix it could provide. When justified, include one strong customer result. Make a direct but low-friction ask.

Position Zendesk as a service layer, operating view, or workflow capability that can work with existing systems. Avoid implying that the prospect needs to replace its broader architecture.

Email 3 must stand alone. Do not drop a product capability into the sequence without explaining why it matters to this recipient and this business problem.

### Email 4

Use direct qualification when the sequence needs a final answer. Restate the relevant business issue or initiative so the email can be read alone. Ask whether the issue is relevant, owned elsewhere, already covered, or not important enough to pursue. Make it easy for the prospect to disqualify the thread.

## Cadence Rule

The four emails above are the canonical writing architecture. They do not prescribe a universal send cadence.

Use three to five email opportunities inside an adaptive, multichannel account motion. Select LinkedIn, phone, referrals, or other approved channels based on account type, persona, trigger strength, engagement, reply behavior, and stop rules. Never add touches solely to complete a sequence.

## Proof Point Rules

Match proof to the business pressure, not simply the industry.

Use:

- Dutch Bros for employee service and manual process pressure
- Enercity for automation and AI resolution
- KFC UK for first-contact resolution and customer satisfaction
- GLL for call volume and resolution speed
- Dunlop Sports for abandonment, answer time, and service levels
- Lush for retail service, first-contact resolution, and cost reduction

Proof points belong in Email 2 or Email 3 by default. A controlled Email 1 test may use one matched proof point when it clarifies relevance without turning the message into a pitch.

## Language Rules

Every message must:

- Use plain language
- Prefer contractions
- Sound like a person testing a relevant business question, not a campaign template
- Use specific company or operating context before generic profile personalization
- Use one specific person-level observation when it sharpens the business point
- Use natural first-person language when it adds credibility or warmth
- Offer a concrete support path or fix once the message reaches the Zendesk point of view
- Use one credible reason for the outreach
- Make the CTA easy to answer, including a useful “not a priority” response
- Avoid corporate filler
- Avoid passive voice when active language works
- Contain no em dashes
- Contain no emojis
- Avoid banned phrases such as “boost,” “supercharge,” “revolutionize,” “turbocharge,” “swiftly,” “leverage,” “optimize,” “streamline,” “synergy,” and “solution”
- Avoid “I hope this finds you well”
- Avoid “I wanted to reach out”
- Avoid “companies like yours”
- Avoid unsupported technology claims
- Avoid generic pain statements
- Avoid telling the recipient what their job is
- Avoid broad questions that ask the recipient to explain their entire function
- Avoid choppy follow-ups that require the earlier email for context
- Avoid “I’d love to connect” unless the message has already earned the conversation
- Avoid “let me know if you’re interested” when a more specific question would produce a better reply
- Avoid generic “following up” subjects when the message can name the issue, account, or reason for contact
- Avoid stacking multiple capabilities or business problems into one email

## Final Audit

Before delivering the emails, check:

- Was the account researched first?
- Are the core signals sourced?
- Are facts separated from inferences?
- Does each prospect map to one signal?
- Does each email fit the prospect’s actual role?
- Do Emails 2 through 4 make sense if read without the earlier emails?
- Does each follow-up add a new implication rather than repeat the first touch?
- Does the Zendesk email offer a practical support path tied to the business issue?
- Does the copy use first-person language naturally without narrating the sender’s research process?
- Does the copy address the recipient directly with natural personal pronouns where useful?
- Does the copy pass the read-aloud phone test?
- Does the opening contain one distinctive observation rather than generic personalization or a profile summary?
- Does the question validate a possible operating consequence instead of exploiting or asserting unverified pain?
- If the copy compares the account with competitors, is the comparison supported by comparable evidence?
- Does the copy avoid telling the prospect what their job is and avoid broad questions?
- Is the opening specific?
- Is the pressure plausible but not overstated?
- Is Email 1 clearly using the default product-neutral approach or a deliberate vendor-present test?
- If Email 1 names Zendesk or uses proof, is it limited to one short, relevant sentence with no feature list or meeting ask?
- Is the CTA focused on a reply?
- Does the message explain why the recipient should care before asking for time?
- Is the reason for outreach based on account context, a verified relationship, relevant proof, or a clear prior interaction?
- Does the tone feel conversational and low-pressure without becoming vague?
- Does the message contain one clear idea and one useful question?
- Is any permission-based language supported by the actual message?
- Is any proof point matched to the prospect's business pressure?
- Does the final email make disqualification easy?
- Is the body normally 50 to 100 words, or does any longer version contain necessary, verified context without adding a second idea?
- Is the subject within the word limit?
- Are all banned phrases removed?
- Are there no em dashes or emojis?
- Are all unverified claims clearly flagged?

## Required Inputs For A New Account

Provide:

- Account name
- Company website or research materials, if available
- Account research document or links
- LinkedIn prospect document after the account research is complete

The work should then proceed in this order:

Account research → signal map → prospect recommendations → LinkedIn context → personalized Email 1 drafts → follow-up sequence.
