# Email Outreach Drafting Standard

## Canonical Status

This file defines the canonical four-email writing architecture for enterprise outbound. Four emails are the writing structure, not a universal cadence guarantee.

The broader cadence is adaptive and multichannel. Use three to five email opportunities based on account type, persona, trigger strength, engagement, reply behavior, and stop rules. Coordinate email with LinkedIn, phone, referrals, or other approved channels when the account and persona justify it.

Evidence boundary:

- Treat short, relevant, non-promotional first touches and interest-based CTAs as evidence-backed defaults.
- Treat account-first research, the four-email architecture, proof timing, and channel mix as operating standards or testable hypotheses unless a cited study directly supports the specific claim.
- Measure positive replies, qualified conversations, meetings, opportunities, and unwanted-contact signals. Do not treat opens as a reliable success metric.

## Purpose

Write prospecting sequences that make the sender useful and worth replying to.

The email should show that we understand:

1. What may be changing or creating pressure at the company.
2. What operational consequence may follow from that change.
3. Why the prospect’s role makes their perspective relevant.
4. Why Zendesk could be a credible consideration.

The goal is not to prove that a problem exists. The goal is to form a grounded hypothesis and ask a sharp question that the prospect can answer.

## Canonical Message Logic

Every sequence should follow this logic:

`Company context or change -> operational consequence -> role relevance -> Zendesk hypothesis -> curious question`

Do not begin with the LinkedIn profile and stop there. A profile is supporting evidence, not the reason for the outreach.

## Source Hierarchy

Use sources in this order:

1. Account context and company research.
2. Current company changes, growth, transformation, restructuring, technology initiatives, acquisitions, geographic expansion, or operating-model changes.
3. Prospect role, responsibilities, recent move, public statement, or relevant activity.
4. Product and service context that supports the Zendesk hypothesis.

Keep sourced facts separate from inference. Do not present a hypothesis as a known company priority.

If no meaningful company change or pressure is available, say so internally and use a clearly labeled hypothesis. Do not invent a trigger.

## What Good Personalization Means

Personalization is not repeating the prospect’s title or listing their career history.

Good personalization connects a specific company situation to the prospect’s likely operating responsibility.

Weak:

> Your profile spans digital, operations, and transformation.

Stronger:

> As Vans moves strategic choices into execution across a global brand, the harder question may be whether service demand stays visible across markets and teams.

The prospect should feel that the email could only reasonably have been written for their company and role.

## Zendesk Relevance

Do not force Zendesk into every first email. The commercial connection must become clear across the sequence.

Zendesk may be relevant when the account needs to:

- Give customer, employee, partner, or internal service teams one place to manage requests.
- Improve visibility across channels, regions, functions, or business units.
- Make self-service easier to find and measure.
- Route work consistently while preserving appropriate ownership.
- Identify recurring service issues that reveal process or product friction.
- Connect service signals to operations, product, HR, digital, or transformation decisions.
- Automate repetitive interactions while routing exceptions to people.
- Improve service without requiring every underlying system to be replaced.

Use only the capability that fits the prospect’s role and the account hypothesis. Do not list product features.

## Sequence Structure

Write four canonical emails per prospect. Each email must stand alone if the prospect never read the prior message. The live cadence may use fewer or more email opportunities when account context, engagement, or stop rules justify it.

### Email 1: Account hypothesis

Purpose:

- Establish the company context.
- Connect it to a plausible operating consequence.
- Ask one focused question.

Do not over-explain Zendesk in the first email. The message should create relevance without sounding like a product pitch.

### Email 2: Operational implication

Purpose:

- Introduce a new implication of the company change.
- Make the issue concrete in the prospect’s area of responsibility.
- Ask how the company sees or manages that issue today.

Do not restate Email 1. Do not reference the act of sending a prior email.

### Email 3: Zendesk point of view

Purpose:

- State the specific Zendesk capability that may be relevant.
- Explain the operational problem it addresses.
- Ask how the prospect thinks about that problem.

Use plain language. For example:

> Zendesk can help teams bring those interactions into one operating view and see where service is slowing execution.

Do not make unsupported claims about results, customers, benchmarks, or implementation.

### Email 4: Direct qualification

Purpose:

- Make the relevance decision easy.
- Ask whether the issue matters, belongs elsewhere, or is not a priority.
- End the sequence cleanly without manufactured urgency.

Do not use a fake breakup, guilt, or pressure. A direct disqualification question is more credible.

## Voice

The sender should sound like a thoughtful operator who understands the prospect’s world.

Use:

- Specific observations.
- Plain language.
- Short paragraphs.
- One idea per email.
- One primary question.
- Calm confidence.
- Genuine curiosity.
- Commercial relevance without a hard pitch.

Avoid:

- BDR language.
- Sales jargon.
- Empty pleasantries.
- Overly polished marketing language.
- Excessive enthusiasm.
- Unproven claims.
- Generic compliments.
- Artificial urgency.

## Banned Phrases and Patterns

Never use:

- “Following up on my note.”
- “Just checking in.”
- “Bumping this up.”
- “Wanted to circle back.”
- “Touching base.”
- “Hope you are well.”
- “I know you are busy.”
- “Close the loop.”
- “A useful starting point is usually...”
- “Whoever owns it...”
- “Is this on your radar?” unless the phrase is genuinely necessary and made specific.

Do not use a generic reference to the prospect’s profile as the main reason for writing.

Do not use “stood out,” “caught my attention,” or similar praise unless followed immediately by a specific company or operating implication.

Do not ask for a meeting before earning the right to ask for one.

## Question Standards

Every question should reveal something about the prospect’s operating reality.

Good questions ask about:

- Where work becomes visible.
- How teams distinguish isolated issues from recurring patterns.
- Which handoffs create delay or rework.
- How the company chooses automation targets.
- How service signals influence product, operations, HR, or transformation decisions.
- Which team owns the end-to-end experience.

Avoid questions that only ask for routing:

> Does another team own this?

If ownership matters, first ask about the operating problem, then use ownership as a secondary path.

## Length and Editing

Every word must earn its place.

Before finalizing, remove:

- Repeated context.
- Unnecessary adjectives.
- Generic setup language.
- Product terminology that does not clarify the point.
- Second questions that dilute the first.
- Claims that are not supported by the source material.

Prefer one strong observation and one strong question over three weaker observations.

## Delegated Drafting Standard

When delegated drafting or sub-agents are available:

1. Give each drafter the canonical instructions in this file.
2. Give the drafter only the relevant account and prospect context.
3. Require a four-email sequence with a distinct purpose for each email.
4. Require the drafter to identify the company change, operational consequence, role relevance, and Zendesk hypothesis before writing.
5. Review every draft against the same canonical instructions.
6. Rewrite any message that drifts into profile summary, generic follow-up language, product dumping, or unsupported claims.

Delegation is for parallel drafting. It does not replace the final editorial pass.

Use this structure as a starting point, not as copy to repeat mechanically.

## Final QA Checklist

Before delivery, verify every prospect sequence:

- Does Email 1 contain company context, not only profile context?
- Is there a credible change, pressure, or operating hypothesis?
- Is the prospect’s role relevant to the question?
- Does each email add new information?
- Can each email stand alone?
- Is the Zendesk connection clear by Email 3 at the latest?
- Is Zendesk presented as a possible fit, not an assumed answer?
- Does each email contain one primary question?
- Does the question invite insight rather than merely route to another person?
- Are all claims supported or clearly framed as hypotheses?
- Are there zero banned phrases?
- Is there no BDR filler or jargon?
- Are subjects specific, readable, and correctly capitalized?
- Is the sequence ordered correctly under the right prospect?
- Does the final email make disqualification easy?
- Would a credible operator consider the message useful even without buying?

## Quality Bar

Do not deliver a sequence merely because it is complete.

The sequence is ready only when it makes the prospect think:

> This person understands what may be changing here, why it could affect my team, and why Zendesk might be worth considering.
