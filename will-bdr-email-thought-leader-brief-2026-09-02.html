BDR email writing: what the strongest public frameworks agree on

A comparison of Josh Braun, Becc Holland, Sam McKenna, Will Allred, and Armand Farrokh against Will Richardson's canonical Zendesk outreach rules.

Bottom line: Your standard already captures the strongest consensus: relevant context, one problem, plain language, calm curiosity, and a low-friction question. The material disagreements are when to name the vendor, when to use proof, whether personal research or account research leads, and how short the first email should be.

What the chart says

Consensus worth keeping

  • Start with something relevant to the buyer.
  • Tie personalization to a real business problem.
  • Use one idea, plain words, and a human tone.
  • Ask one easy question before asking for time.

Real disagreement

  • Your rules ban Zendesk and proof from Email 1.
  • Braun and 30MPC often include a concise solution or proof in Email 1.
  • Sam and Becc give personal research more weight than your account-first hierarchy.
  • Published length advice ranges from 25 to 100 words.

Top takeaways from each voice

Josh Braun

Neutral curiosity
  • Context must answer why this person, not merely repeat a profile fact.
  • Specific language is more believable than polished generalities.
  • Ask neutral questions that let the prospect reject the premise.
  • Gauge interest before asking for time.
  • Treat every sales rule as a perspective to test.

Best contribution to your rules: sharper neutrality and a stronger experiment mindset.

Becc Holland

Personalization plus relevance
  • Personalization is one-to-one. Relevance is one-to-many.
  • Role relevance is table stakes. Firm, technology, and trigger context make it sharper.
  • The personal first line must connect to a diagnostic question.
  • Ask about a measurable unknown, not a vague adjective.
  • Minimize seller-company mentions.

Best contribution to your rules: explicitly distinguish true personalization from segmented relevance.

Sam McKenna

Show Me You Know Me
  • Research should make the buyer feel understood, not surveilled.
  • The subject and first line should prove the message was written for them.
  • Authentic human connection matters more than a polished template.
  • The ask should follow earned relevance.
  • Follow-ups should stay thoughtful and never sound desperate.

Best contribution to your rules: preserve voice consistency after the first touch, not only in Email 1.

Will Allred

Observed email patterns
  • Shorter emails reduce reading effort. Lavender recommends 25 to 50 words.
  • One idea beats multiple claims.
  • Short words and concrete language beat buzzwords.
  • Tone and proof should change by department and seniority.
  • Benchmarks should be contextual, not universal.

Best contribution to your rules: add segment-level testing and clearer editing targets.

Armand Farrokh

Trigger to problem bridge
  • Personalization must explain why the buyer may have the problem.
  • Use four parts: personalization, problem, minimum solution, and low-friction CTA.
  • Write in short mobile paragraphs and human language.
  • Ask for interest before calendar logistics.
  • Follow-ups can reuse the same thread, then add proof and a useful offer.

Best contribution to your rules: make the bridge from signal to operational consequence explicit.

Recommended source-of-truth stack

1
Your measured outcomes by segment.
Positive replies, qualified conversations, meetings, opportunities, and unwanted-contact signals. These should update the rules.
2
Your canonical rules.
Use them as the default operating standard until a controlled test produces better evidence.
3
Published data with a visible method.
Use Lavender and Gong-style datasets as directional evidence, not universal causation.
4
Thought-leader frameworks.
Use them to generate tests. Do not elevate a creator's self-reported result into a rule.

Evidence limits

There is no neutral, comprehensive ranking of BDR email thought leaders. These five were selected because each has a distinct, documented framework and active public material. Most performance claims are creator-controlled or vendor-controlled. Lavender's 2026 sample is large, but it is observational and drawn from Lavender-connected inboxes. Sam McKenna's 7x claim and Becc Holland's 35 SQL claim do not publish enough method to serve as causal proof.
